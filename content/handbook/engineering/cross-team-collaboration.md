## Overview

Working across teams on a project or initiative can be challenging. Teams have their own ways of working, have developed processes and subcultures unique to them that drive their efficiency, and are used to delivering as a single unit. When team members from multiple teams need to get together to work on something, an environment is created that is ripe for conflict, confusion, frustration, and ultimately failing to deliver results and [engineering excellence](_index.md#engineering-excellence) for our customers.

This guide provides some advice and warning signs to consider when working in a cross-team collaborative project.

GitLab's [Collaboration value](../values/_index.md#collaboration) provides a framework for success. Many of the topics below fall directly into some of the Collaboration sub-values: [kindness](../values/_index.md#kindness), [get to know each other](../values/_index.md#get-to-know-each-other), [assume positive intent](../values/_index.md#assume-positive-intent), [don't let each other fail](../values/_index.md#dont-let-each-other-fail), [short toes](../values/_index.md#short-toes), and more. This guide does not attempt to supersede or modify those, but describe how they can look in the scope of a cross-team collaborative effort.

## Traits of successful collaboration

All collaborations are different, but these guidelines may help drive success.

### Work as a unified team

When it comes to delivering a shared cross-team project. The people involved need to be working as and viewed as a single team within the scope of that project. This brings a shared and equal level of responsibility, commitment, and achievement for everyone involved.

### Create a shared objective

If each team has their own individual objective, then the teams are not working as one. A shared objective needs to be developed. This creates opportunities to communicate and collaborate more. Similar to GitLab's value around [Results for Customers](../values/_index.md#results), the shared objective should allow the teams to use a common language to communicate, motivate, and stay focused on the ultimate goal. A due date will help for this shared objective will help create motivation. Make sure it's ambitious to enable a [sense of urgency](../values/_index.md#sense-of-urgency), but not aggressive.

### Understand each others processes

When teams come together to work on something, often the first problems arise when they realize they do things differently. Maybe they have a different review process, a different way of communicating status, different expectations on how work is broken down and assigned. Early on, take the time to share processes. When there are differences, there are a few ways to approach resolving them:

1. Pick one team's process and stick with it.

   This may cause some frustration for other teams involved, but if it's made clear and optimally there is a reason why one team's process was chosen over another's then team members will find ways to [disagree and commit](../values/_index.md#disagree-and-commit).

2. Develop a shared process where needed.

   In some cases, a shared process may need to be created that is different from any one team's process. Make sure it is [documented somewhere](../values/_index.md#write-things-down) and everyone is aware of it so it can easily be referenced as team members get used to it.

3. Agree to continue with separate processes in some areas.

   Sometimes it may make sense to keep separate processes. For example, if two projects are being contributed to that each have different review processes, it may make sense to keep the review process for each project when contributing to that particular project.

The main thing here is that everyone involved needs to understand what the processes and expectations are to avoid going down paths of conflict and frustration.

### Identify guardrails, but not boundaries

When starting a cross-team collaboration, it is important to define the roles and responsibilities of each group. It is also a good idea to have everyone develop a shared understanding of each team member and team's capabilities. We want to be careful to not set up boundaries. Boundaries say "you need to work on this, but cannot work on that". Guardrails say "you are responsible for this, but can contribute anywhere that you can help". In large projects, different people involved will have different amounts and complexity of work to complete over time. The goal is to deliver the results for customers, so if that means stepping across team lines to contribute along the way, that is how guardrails can help.

### Have subject matter experts available

When several team members from multiple teams are driving a project forward, there is opportunity for misalignment. It is easy for team members to execute their path of work while not realizing how any decisions or changes may align with the scope outside the project. Having subject matter experts available even when they are not directly involved in the work related to the project provides a way for those with higher level perspective to weigh in and ensure the project does not become misaligned. [Staff+ engineers](careers/matrix/staff.md) are good candidates to fill this role in an advisory sense where they attend meetings, provide feedback on decisions, design docs, or any changes to scope without actively participating in the project. Having these people identified provides an understanding of who people within the project can reach out to.

### Include all stakeholders

Ensure all stakeholders are fully engaged early and throughout, including vendors. When different stakeholders have different priorities and expectations around a shared project, it provides opportunity for missing key information that could lead to solving the wrong problem or building something that just won't work or fulfill customer needs. Stakeholders not only need to be involved, but need to have a mutual understanding of the [shared objective](#create-a-shared-objective) and priority of that project at the level that all teams are involved be it group, department, division, or company.

### Find ways to have fun

Individual teams build trust over time through social and work interaction every day. When put into an environment where many team members have never worked together before but are expected to have a high degree of overlap, it is important to find ways to quickly build trust. This could be having a regular demo call if timezones allow for it. Demos are a great way to prompt discussion and make the feedback loop more personable, which can build the trust needed to collaborate on a cross-team project. As trust grows, these can scale down, but remember that while everyone assumes positive intent, it is natural for each team to not understand what other teams do, why it takes so much time, why they've developed certain processes and working habits. Taking the time to break down these barriers at the start of a cross-team collaboration will contribute to greater efficiency and general happiness of the teams over the course of the project.

## Warning signs

When collaboration across teams deteriorates or is in an unhealthy state, there are some warning signs to look out for.

### Differing communication between teams

When teams work together on a project, when it comes to the ceremonies and types of communication happening related to that project, they need to act as one team. When teams are communicating in isolation rather than together on a project, that is a sign that communication behaviors need to change. Otherwise there's risk of divergent work and general frustration growing between teams.

### Clashing over responsibilities

Everyone involved is responsible for delivering the project. When there is conflict over who should be doing what, that is a sign that the initial guardrails and expectations were not correctly set or communicated. As managers of one, it is everyone's responsibility to ensure that enabling each other always comes first. Different teams will have different roles, but ultimately the project needs to be delivered, so whoever is capable and able should be willing to help when it makes sense to do so.
